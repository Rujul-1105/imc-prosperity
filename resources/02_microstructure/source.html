The canonical books and papers that teach this module. Read the chapters listed; skip the rest.

Main source (read this first)

Larry Harris, Trading and Exchanges: Market Microstructure for Practitioners, Oxford University Press 2003.

This is the single best book on the topic. Harris was chief economist at the SEC; he knows both the market-maker and the regulator side. It's used as a textbook in most quant programs.

Read: Chapters 1–7, 12–14. Skim the rest.

  • Ch 1 — The trading problem. Why exchanges exist.
  • Ch 2 — Orders, quotes, market structure. (Basic vocabulary.)
  • Ch 3 — The limit order book. (How a book works.)
  • Ch 4 — Dealers and dealers markets.
  • Ch 5 — Brokers and auction markets.
  • Ch 6 — Market makers and specialist markets.
  • Ch 7 — Information, quotes, and trading. (Adverse selection explained.)
  • Ch 12 — The technology of trading. (How matching engines work.)
  • Ch 13 — Trade reporting. (The data you get from a feed.)
  • Ch 14 — Transaction costs and order types. (Market vs limit, etc.)

Where to get it:

  • OUP print: ~$80 (worth it for the marginalia).
  • USC faculty page (lharris.usc.edu): the original book chapters are posted as free PDFs. The canonical link is "Trading and Exchanges / Book Chapters" — Google "Harris Trading and Exchanges market microstructure book chapters PDF" if the link moves.
  • Library: most university libraries have it.

Supplement 1 — BIS microstructure paper

Mahmoodzadeh & Marmiroli (or similar BIS authors), "Order flow and the bid–ask spread in modern markets," BIS Working Paper.

Short, math-light, modern. Good for visualizing order book heatmaps and thinking about queue priority in modern pro-rata markets. bis.org → search "market microstructure."

Supplement 2 — Timo Diehm's code

github.com/TimoDiehm/imc-prosperity-3 — Prosperity 3, 2nd place.

Not a book. The code is the source. Read round_1_strategy.py and trace how his OrderBook class parses the CSV. Compare with your code/order_book.py in this module.

What this module does NOT cover (and where to look)

  • Stochastic order book models (Cont, Bouchaud) — advanced, skip until module 17.
  • Queue priority variants (pro-rata, FIFO, hybrid) — module 17.
  • Latency arbitrage — module 17.
  • Adverse selection models (Glosten–Milgrom, Kyle) — module 17.

Reading time budget

Harris Ch 1–7: 6 hours. Ch 12–14: 3 hours. Supplement 1: 1 hour. Supplement 2: 2 hours tracing code. Total: ~12 hours. Spread over the week.

Once you've read these, move to extract.html for the formulas and code.
