Microstructure — the plain-English tour

No math, no jargon. Imagine explaining to a friend who's never traded.

The story

Imagine a farmer's market where everyone wants to trade apples. There's a big chalkboard. On the left side, in green, people write down how many apples they want to buy and at what price. On the right side, in red, people write how many they want to sell and at what price.

Say Alice writes "Buy 10 at $1.00." Bob writes "Buy 5 at $1.00." Charlie writes "Sell 4 at $1.05."

When a customer walks up and says "I'll buy 4 apples," the system matches them to the cheapest seller. Charlie sells 4 at $1.05. If the customer wanted 8, Charlie sells 4 and then the system goes to the next-cheapest seller (say David at $1.10) for the remaining 4.

That's an order book.

Bid, ask, mid, spread

  • Bid = the highest price anyone is willing to pay to buy. From the example: $1.00 (from Alice and Bob).
  • Ask = the lowest price anyone is willing to accept to sell. From the example: $1.05 (from Charlie).
  • Mid = (bid + ask) / 2. From the example: ($1.00 + $1.05) / 2 = $1.025. The "center" of the market.
  • Spread = ask − bid. From the example: $0.05. The cost of round-tripping.

The market-maker's whole job is to post both a bid and an ask, get hit on whichever side, and pocket the spread. They are the green and red writing on the chalkboard.

Depth

Now imagine 50 people all wrote "Buy 10 at $0.99." That's a wall — 500 apples of demand at almost the same price. If you walk up trying to sell 100 apples, the chalkboard absorbs the first 500 of demand easily and the price barely moves. That's a deep book.

If only 5 people wrote "Buy 10 at $0.99," then 50 apples of demand. Your 100-apple sell will eat through all of it and then crash the price down to whoever's next. That's a thin book.

Depth = how much volume is sitting at each price level. Deep = liquid = prices move slowly. Thin = illiquid = prices move fast.

Microprice

Imagine the bid is $1.00 with 500 apples behind it, and the ask is $1.05 with only 5 apples behind it. The "mid" is $1.025. But the real center of gravity is closer to $1.00 — there's a lot of demand, very little supply, the price should be a tiny bit above $1.00, not $1.025.

The microprice is a smarter "center" that leans toward the side with more volume:

microprice = (bid_volume × ask + ask_volume × bid) / (bid_volume + ask_volume)

In our example: (500 × $1.05 + 5 × $1.00) / 505 = ($525 + $5) / 505 = $530 / 505 ≈ $1.0495. That's the better "fair value" estimate.

Timo Diehm's Wall-Mid strategy uses the microprice as the center of where to post his quotes. See the case study.

Queue priority

Back to the chalkboard. Say three people all wrote "Buy 10 at $1.00" at different times — Alice first, then Bob, then Charlie. If a seller shows up, who gets filled first?

Most exchanges say: price-time priority. Best price first; among equal prices, the one who wrote it first. So Alice fills first, then Bob, then Charlie.

But Prosperity's exchange uses a different rule: no queue priority. Whoever happens to match gets matched. This is the "1-tick-better-pricing" alpha — if you submit a buy at $1.005 when everyone else is at $1.00, you get filled even though you're last in line.

This is huge. It means in Prosperity you can always out-prioritize the existing book by 1 tick. The catch: if you're 1 tick better, you're paying more / getting less, so the spread you capture is 1 tick tighter. The strategy works when 1 tick is < your expected edge.

Market vs limit orders

Two ways to enter the market:

  • Market order: "I want to buy right now at whatever the cheapest seller asks." Fills immediately, but you pay the spread. Used when you have strong information and need to act fast.
  • Limit order: "I want to buy at $1.00 or cheaper. If no one sells that cheap, leave my order on the board." Doesn't fill unless the price reaches your level. Used when you want to provide liquidity and earn the spread.

Prosperity supports both, with the rule above (no queue priority). The whole point of market-making strategies is to use limit orders as the maker and capture the spread.

Inventory and PnL

Your position (also called inventory) is how much of the asset you currently hold. If you've bought 50 and sold 30, you're long 20. Long = positive position. Short = negative position.

Your PnL (profit and loss) is what you'd make if you flattened your position right now at the current mid price:

PnL = sum of (sell_price − buy_price) for closed trades + current_position × (current_mid − average_entry_price)

Prosperity ranks you by PnL. Higher PnL = better rank.

Adverse selection, finally

Imagine you're a market-maker posting a bid of $1.00 and an ask of $1.05. A trader comes and sells to you at $1.00. Then they immediately come back and buy from you at $1.05. You just made $0.05 — that's your spread.

But if the same trader only does that once, and then stops, and the next trade that comes through is someone selling to you at $1.00 again, and the price then crashes to $0.95… you got adversely selected. The smart trader knew the price was about to fall and used you as the exit. Your $0.05 spread didn't cover the $0.05 loss on the position you were left holding.

Adverse selection is the market-maker's enemy. The Avellaneda–Stoikov formula in module 03 is a way to widen your quotes when adverse selection risk is high.

Pulling it together

The order book is the heart of everything. Every strategy reads it, writes to it, and tries to be one tick better than the next person. If you can read a book and feel what the spread, depth, microprice, and queue priority are telling you, you're ready for the rest of the curriculum.
