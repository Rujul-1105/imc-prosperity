Mean Reversion — the plain-English tour

What it means for a price to "want to go back", and how to bet on it.

← module README · source · extract · exercises

The story

Imagine a ball on a stretched rubber sheet. The ball is at some position. There's a dent in the middle of the sheet. If you flick the ball, it wobbles around the dent, eventually settling back to the bottom.

That's mean reversion. A price (or any variable) that has a "preferred" value (the dent, the mean) and is pulled back toward it when it strays. The pull can be weak (slow reversion) or strong (fast reversion), but it's there.

Many real-world financial prices behave this way. The interest rate of a country can't drift arbitrarily far from its long-term average (central banks intervene). The price of cocoa can't permanently decouple from the price of chocolate (they come from the same supply chain). The spread between two cointegrated stocks always eventually closes.

If you can detect mean reversion in a price, you can bet on it: when the price is high above the mean, sell; when it's low, buy. Capture the reversion as profit.

AR(1) — the simplest mean-reverting process

The simplest mathematical model of a mean-reverting price is called AR(1), for "autoregressive of order 1." The model says: the next price depends on the current price plus some noise.

next_price = constant + β × current_price + noise

For mean reversion, β is between -1 and 1. If β is positive and close to 1, the price is persistent (tomorrow's price is similar to today's, no reversion). If β is close to 0, the price has no memory at all (random walk). If β is negative, the price is anti-persistent (a jump up today means a pull-back tomorrow). Most "mean-reverting" prices in finance have β slightly positive but with a strong pull toward the mean — the "constant" term is non-trivial.

The fitted model can be rewritten as:

(next_price − mean) = β × (current_price − mean) + noise

where the mean = constant / (1 − β). When β is between 0 and 1, "next minus mean" is a fraction of "current minus mean" plus noise. The deviations from the mean shrink geometrically over time. That's mean reversion.

The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

AR(1) is the discrete-time version. The continuous-time version is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process:

dS = θ × (μ − S) dt + σ dW

Translated: the change in S over an instant is proportional to how far S is from μ (the long-term mean), with θ being the strength of the pull, plus random noise. When S = μ, the deterministic part is zero — the price wanders randomly around the mean. When S > μ, the deterministic part is negative — the price is pulled down. When S < μ, the deterministic part is positive — the price is pulled up.

The discrete approximation of OU (at time step Δt) is the AR(1) model. The two are equivalent for small Δt.

Half-life

The most useful summary of a mean-reverting process is its half-life: how long it typically takes for a deviation to shrink by half.

For AR(1) with parameter β (0 < β < 1), the half-life is:

half_life = −log(2) / log(β) = log(0.5) / log(β)

If β = 0.95, half-life ≈ 13.5 steps. If β = 0.99, half-life ≈ 69 steps. If β = 0.5, half-life ≈ 1 step.

In Prosperity tick data, a "step" is one tick of the price series (whatever the round's data granularity is — usually per simulated tick).

Why half-life matters: it tells you how long you have to wait for the mean reversion to pay off. If your z-score entry threshold is 2 standard deviations, and half-life is 5 ticks, you expect to be in the trade for ~5 ticks to capture half the move (z goes from 2 to 1). If half-life is 50 ticks, you're in the trade 10× longer, with 10× the opportunity cost and risk of adverse moves.

Z-score

A z-score is "how many standard deviations is the current value from the mean?" Standardize the price series:

z = (current_price − mean) / standard_deviation

If z = 0, the price is at the mean. If z = +2, it's two standard deviations above. If z = −2, two below.

For a mean-reverting series, z tends to return to 0 over time. So the simple strategy is:

  • z > +2: sell (price is high; expect reversion down).
  • z < −2: buy (price is low; expect reversion up).
  • |z| < 0.5: close the position (we're at the mean, no edge).

The threshold (2 in this example) is a parameter. Lower threshold → more trades, less profit per trade, more exposure to noise. Higher threshold → fewer trades, more profit per trade, longer holding periods.

Failure modes

Mean reversion fails in two ways:

  1. The jump is too big. If a price moves 10 standard deviations in one tick (a "fat tail"), the z-score is huge, but the next move isn't necessarily a reversion — it might be the start of a new regime. The model breaks down at the tails, exactly where you'd bet the most.
  2. The process isn't actually mean-reverting. If you fit an AR(1) on a trending series, you get β close to 1 (high persistence, no reversion). The model says "wait for reversion" and the trend continues. You lose.

Leo Hawking's rule: "Statistical models fail where you need them most." The z-score is high precisely when the model is most uncertain. Trust the model at low z, distrust it at high z.

Connection to Prosperity

STARFRUIT, ASH, and FRUIT are all mean-reverting in P3 and P4. The team's prior P4 work used z-score mean reversion on HYDROGEL_PACK and it worked in Round 3. The discipline is to fit the AR(1) on past data, estimate the half-life, and pick a threshold that gives you positive expected PnL after transaction costs.
