Avellaneda–Stoikov (2008) — the formula

The setup. The market-maker:

  • Posts a bid and an ask.
  • Holds an inventory $q$ (positive = long, negative = short).
  • Wants to maximize expected utility of terminal PnL, penalized by inventory variance.
  • Believes the mid follows arithmetic Brownian motion with volatility $\sigma$.

The solution gives two quantities:

Reservation price: $r(s, q, t) = s - q \cdot \gamma \cdot \sigma^2 \cdot (T - t)$

Optimal half-spread: $\delta = \frac{\gamma \cdot \sigma^2 \cdot (T - t)}{2} + \frac{2}{\gamma} \cdot \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{\gamma}{\kappa}\right)$

Then the market-maker posts:

$\text{bid} = r - \delta$
$\text{ask} = r + \delta$

Substituting:

$\text{bid} = s - q \cdot \gamma \cdot \sigma^2 \cdot (T-t) - \frac{\gamma \sigma^2 (T-t)}{2} - \frac{2}{\gamma} \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{\gamma}{\kappa}\right)$
$\text{ask} = s - q \cdot \gamma \cdot \sigma^2 \cdot (T-t) + \frac{\gamma \sigma^2 (T-t)}{2} + \frac{2}{\gamma} \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{\gamma}{\kappa}\right)$

What each term does

TermEffectDirection
$s$Mid price. The "center" of the quote.Reference
$-q \cdot \gamma \cdot \sigma^2 \cdot (T-t)$Inventory penalty. Long → lower reservation, encouraging sells.Skew
$\pm \frac{\gamma \sigma^2 (T-t)}{2}$Volatility-adjusted half-spread. Higher vol → wider quote.Spread
$\pm \frac{2}{\gamma} \ln(1 + \gamma/\kappa)$Constant offset based on order flow.Spread

The five parameters

1. $s$ — mid price

Either the regular mid $(p^b + p^a)/2$ or the microprice. The microprice is a better estimate of the true mid. Use it.

2. $q$ — current inventory

Read from your own position state. Update every tick. Positive = long.

3. $\sigma$ — volatility

Estimate from rolling realized vol over the past $N$ ticks. $N = 50$ is a good default. Per-tick volatility:

$\sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(\Delta p_i) \text{ over past } N \text{ ticks}}$

For Prosperity tick data, $\Delta p_i$ is the per-tick mid change.

4. $\gamma$ — risk aversion

How much you penalize holding inventory. Bigger $\gamma$ → wider spread, less inventory. Tune by sweeping a grid:

  1. Run a backtest with $\gamma = 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10$.
  2. For each, measure the Sharpe ratio of the strategy.
  3. Pick the $\gamma$ with the highest Sharpe.
  4. Repeat for each round (the optimal $\gamma$ is round-specific).

Rule of thumb: $\gamma$ between $0.01$ and $1$ for typical Prosperity products.

5. $\kappa$ — order arrival rate

How often orders arrive at the touch per unit time. Estimate from historical fill rate:

$\kappa = \frac{\text{number of fills at touch}}{\text{time at touch}}$

For Prosperity: count how many times in past days your touch orders were filled, divided by the total time. Re-estimate per round.

Rule of thumb: $\kappa$ between $0.5$ and $5$ for typical Prosperity products.

6. $T - t$ — time to terminal

In the continuous-time model, $T - t$ is the time horizon. In tick-by-tick backtesting, set:

$T - t = \frac{\text{ticks remaining in round}}{\text{total ticks per round}} \times \text{horizon seconds}$

The horizon seconds is round-specific. P3 R1 was ~1 hour per day (3,600 seconds).

Note: many practitioners set $T - t$ to a small constant (like 60 seconds) and ignore the time decay. The result is a steady-state quote that doesn't widen as $t \to T$. This is fine in practice.

Code: Avellaneda–Stoikov in Python

import math

def as_quotes(mid, inventory, sigma, tau, gamma, kappa):
    """
    Avellaneda-Stoikov (2008) market-making quotes.

    mid:       current mid (or microprice) of the asset
    inventory: signed inventory (positive = long)
    sigma:     per-unit-time volatility
    tau:       T - t, time to terminal (same units as 1/sigma^2)
    gamma:     risk aversion parameter
    kappa:     order arrival rate (per unit time)

    Returns: (bid, ask)
    """
    reservation = mid - inventory * gamma * sigma**2 * tau
    half_spread = (gamma * sigma**2 * tau) / 2 + (2 / gamma) * math.log(1 + gamma / kappa)
    return reservation - half_spread, reservation + half_spread

Wall-Mid (Timo Diehm) — the practice

The Wall-Mid heuristic. No math, just read the book.

  1. Find the largest buy wall: the price level with the most volume waiting to buy.
  2. Find the largest sell wall: the price level with the most volume waiting to sell.
  3. Post your bid 1 tick inside the buy wall (so you fill before the wall does).
  4. Post your ask 1 tick outside the sell wall (so you fill when the wall is consumed).
  5. Skew both by inventory: if long, lower both; if short, raise both.

Code (full version in code/wall_mid.py):

def wall_mid_quotes(book, our_position, max_pos=20, skew_per_unit=0.2):
    """
    Wall-Mid: place bid 1 tick inside the largest bid wall, ask 1 tick outside.
    Skew by inventory.
    """
    bids = sorted(book.get("bids", []), key=lambda x: -x[1])
    asks = sorted(book.get("asks", []), key=lambda x: -x[1])
    if not bids or not asks:
        return None, None

    bid_wall_price, _ = bids[0]
    ask_wall_price, _ = asks[0]
    skew = our_position * skew_per_unit

    bid = bid_wall_price + 1 - skew   # 1 tick inside the wall
    ask = ask_wall_price - 1 - skew   # 1 tick outside the wall

    # hard cap
    if our_position >= max_pos:
        bid = None
    elif our_position <= -max_pos:
        ask = None
    return bid, ask

Why "1 tick inside" the wall

The wall is a passive order resting at price $p$ waiting to buy. If you post your bid at $p + 1$, you're offering 1 tick more than the wall. When a market sell comes in, you fill first (at the better price). When the wall finally consumes and the price moves above $p$, your bid becomes stale and you cancel it.

But in Prosperity's no-queue-priority exchange, you also fill first even if you're 1 tick above the wall — so this strategy exploits that property.

Hybrid: Wall-Mid + AS inventory skew

The best market-makers use a hybrid:

  • Center the quote with Wall-Mid (it's the microstructure edge).
  • Skew with the AS inventory term (it's the risk edge).
def hybrid_quotes(book, our_position, mid, sigma, gamma, kappa, max_pos=20):
    """
    Wall-Mid centered, AS-skewed.
    """
    # Wall-Mid center
    bids = sorted(book.get("bids", []), key=lambda x: -x[1])
    asks = sorted(book.get("asks", []), key=lambda x: -x[1])
    if not bids or not asks:
        return None, None
    bid_wall = bids[0][0]
    ask_wall = asks[0][0]
    center = (bid_wall + ask_wall) / 2

    # AS inventory skew
    skew = our_position * gamma * sigma**2

    bid = bid_wall + 1 - skew
    ask = ask_wall - 1 - skew

    if our_position >= max_pos:
        bid = None
    elif our_position <= -max_pos:
        ask = None
    return bid, ask

Backtesting a market-maker

Use the simple event-driven backtester in code/backtest_mm.py. The loop:

  1. For each tick of the historical CSV:
    1. Parse the order book snapshot.
    2. Compute your quotes.
    3. Check if your quotes cross any historical market orders. If yes, fill.
    4. Update inventory and PnL.
  2. At end, return PnL decomposed into: spread capture, inventory drift, rebalance cost.

PnL decomposition

After a backtest, decompose total PnL into three components. This is the standard way to diagnose a market-making strategy:

Spread capture: the cumulative spread earned on round-trip fills.
Inventory drift: PnL from your net position times the price change while you held it.
Rebalance cost: the cost of closing out your final position at the end.

Leo Hawking's retrospective: "If spread capture is negative, the strategy is broken — even if total PnL is positive from drift." Spread capture is the only reliable component. If it's negative, fix the strategy.
